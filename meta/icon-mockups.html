Din · app icon

Four glass directions for the Din icon

The current icon already has the right idea — overlapping waves, lit crests, deep water. What it doesn't have is material, and it is very dark: measured across the icon face it averages 19 out of 255, with 98% of its pixels below a fifth of full brightness. These three directions rebuild it in the glass language macOS 26 and iOS 26 now use — layers that scatter what's behind them, edges that catch light, a contact shadow where each layer meets the one behind it — and they sit about four times brighter. The fourth drops the frost and refracts instead, and one crossbreed sits between two of them.

Tide

Five sheets — the closest read of today's icon

Same five waves you have now, same rhythm, rebuilt as stacked panes of frosted glass. Each sheet blurs and brightens everything behind it, so the water gets progressively milkier toward the front instead of merely lighter. The crest is a hairline specular with a soft bloom under it, and every sheet drops a shadow onto the one behind — that shadow is what sells the layers as separate pieces of material rather than shapes stacked in a single plane.

Reads best at 128px and up. Five layers in a 1024 canvas leaves about 120px of vertical rhythm per sheet. Holding the crests to a 1px floor keeps all five visible right down to 16px — but visible and legible are different things, and five lines stacked in 16 pixels is a comb, not a wave. Beautiful in the Dock, busy in a menu bar.

Swell

Three sheets, deeper water, built to survive 32px

Fewer waves, much bigger. Amplitude roughly doubles and every crest is tilted a few degrees off horizontal, so the icon gains a diagonal it currently lacks — at thumbnail size that diagonal is the whole silhouette. The ground carries the colour here, a warm horizon falling into deep teal, while the glass stays close to neutral. What tints each pane is the light coming through it, not paint on it, which is how Apple's material actually behaves.

The one I'd ship. Still unmistakably the Din icon, still legible at 32px, and at 16px it keeps one crest and a horizon instead of dissolving. The warm-to-cool ground also gives it a place on a crowded Dock that another five-layer blue doesn't get.

Ribbon

Bands rather than sheets — overlap you can actually see

The departure. Instead of filled sheets, three tilted bands of glass float over the ground, each lit on both edges — bright on top, dim underneath, the way a real slab of glass catches a room. Because every band is its own pane, the places where they cross get double blur and double tint, and those crossings become the brightest events in the icon. It's the most literal answer to "waves overlapping each other," and the most current-looking of the three.

The risky one. It trades the water metaphor for a material one — this reads as equaliser more than sea, and the crossings that make it work are gone by 32px. Worth having on the wall, and the direction that would age best if Apple pushes the glass language further.

Current

Variation · Swell × Ribbon
Ribbon's bands at Swell's scale

Ribbon's form — a band of glass with a lit edge top and bottom, tilted off horizontal — scaled up to Swell's proportions and dropped onto Swell's ground. Three bands instead of three sheets, each about a sixth of the canvas thick, with the dusk palette showing through above, between and below them. The top band takes a warm specular off the sky and the lower two stay cool, which is the trick Swell already uses to tie its horizon to its water.

What it gains over Swell is depth: sheets stack, bands float. You can see ground on both sides of every band, so each one reads as a separate piece of material rather than a fill line. What it loses is Swell's silhouette — the strong light-over-dark split at the horizon is gone, replaced by an even rhythm.

The best-looking of the five at full size, and it holds to 32px. Between this and Swell it is genuinely a coin toss: Swell is the bolder thumbnail, Current is the richer icon.

Fetch

Variation · Swell × Ribbon
Ribbon's crest line, filled as a sheet

Ribbon's wave with the band thrown away — only the top line survives, filled downward the way Swell's sheets are, at Swell's size and on Swell's ground. Comparing the two parents' numbers, the cycle counts are almost the same (0.80 / 1.05 / 0.88 against 0.85 / 1.05 / 0.90); what actually separates them is the phase offsets and the rake. Ribbon tilts every wave −7°, where Swell tilts its three by roughly 3°, 2° and 1°, so this reads as Swell with the whole sea lifted toward the right — and the offset phases make the crests cross, which Swell's never do.

Holds as far down as Swell does — at 16px there is still a warm sky over a rising diagonal — and the one consistent rake gives it a cleaner silhouette than Swell's mixed tilts. What it costs is calm: raking every crest the same way reads as motion where Swell reads as horizon. Which of those Din wants is the actual question.

Lens

Clear glass that bends the background instead of covering it

The other three are frosted — they scatter. This one refracts. Each band carries a displacement map built from the slope of a convex edge, so the ground behind it is physically bent: compressed into a bright seam right at the rim, undisturbed through the middle. Three passes at slightly different strengths, recombined one colour channel each, put a faint chromatic fringe on the steepest part of the curve — the tell that separates real glass from a blur.

It only works because the ground is worth looking at. The tint is down to 0.15, so the bands are almost clear; what makes them legible is the distortion at their edges and the light along them. Cover the ground with a flat colour and the whole direction disappears.

The most current-looking, and the least icon-like. The bands themselves survive to 32px on the hairline floor, but refraction is an edge effect measured in fractions of the width, and no floor saves that — by 32px you have the shape without the material. Where it earns its keep is large: an About window, a splash, the App Store listing.

Taking it to code

What makes frost read

Blurring a smooth gradient looks identical to not blurring it. Frost needs something to scatter: coarse texture in the ground that each pane visibly softens, and fine grain inside the pane. Both are feTurbulence, blended as overlay.

Porting to Pillow

Frosted layers are four steps: composite what's below, GaussianBlur it, paste it back through the wave mask, then the specular and the contact shadow. Lens needs one more — a per-pixel remap from the same displacement map, which is numpy fancy-indexing rather than a Pillow call.

Small sizes are redrawn

The ladders are not downscales. Every size is drawn from the same geometry, but a line 0.3% of the icon wide is 1.2px at 320 and 0.1px at 32. Below the hero size the crest, bloom, contact shadow and rim are held to a 1px floor, so the detail stays visible instead of thinning away.

Why Lens is plain SVG

The usual recipe puts the displacement map in backdrop-filter: url(#…), which only Chromium honours. Refracting a copy of the ground inside the icon's own SVG instead costs nothing in fidelity here and renders the same everywhere.

On brightness

The dark variants deliberately start at a mid navy rather than near-black. Measured on the icon face: 19 today; 80–82 for Tide, Swell, Current and Lens; 67 for Ribbon, which trades it for contrast. The light variants sit near 190.

Still to do

macOS 26 also takes Clear and Tinted versions of an icon. Both fall out of whichever direction wins almost for free — drop the ground for Clear, collapse the tints to one hue for Tinted.
